# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Data source and cohort definition

Genomic and phenotypic data were obtained from the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center (BV-BRC, https://www.bv-brc.org, accessed September 2026). We queried all *Escherichia coli* genomes (NCBI Taxonomy ID 562) with an antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) record for ciprofloxacin. The unfiltered query returned 123,469 phenotype records (25,591 resistant, 97,878 susceptible). Because a substantial fraction of AST records in BV-BRC are computationally imputed (evidence type "Computational Method", generated by BV-BRC's internal MIC-prediction model) rather than derived from wet-laboratory susceptibility testing, we restricted the analytic cohort to records with evidence type "Laboratory Method" only, to avoid training and evaluating a genomic prediction model against the output of a different prediction model. This restriction yielded 8,631 phenotype records (2,071 resistant, 6,560 susceptible) corresponding to 7,331 unique genomes.

Of these 7,331 genomes, 1,300 were represented by more than one AST record (typically because the same genome had been cited in more than one susceptibility study). Phenotype labels agreed across all duplicate records for 1,282 genomes, which were collapsed to a single record; the remaining 18 genomes had internally conflicting resistant/susceptible calls across duplicate records and were excluded, leaving 7,313 genomes with an unambiguous ciprofloxacin phenotype label.

Genome-level metadata (isolation country, geographic location, collection year, BioProject accession, multilocus sequence type [MLST], genome assembly status, and genome quality flag) were retrieved separately from BV-BRC's genome metadata table and merged with the phenotype table on the BV-BRC Genome ID.

## 2.2 Quality control

Two additional exclusions were applied prior to analysis. First, BV-BRC-flagged assembly status was used to remove genomes marked "Deprecated" (superseded assembly, n = 1) and genomes with a "Poor" genome-quality flag (n = 38), reflecting known assembly contamination or fragmentation issues that can corrupt automated resistance-gene calling. This yielded a final quality-controlled cohort of 7,274 genomes (1,843 resistant, 5,431 susceptible).

Second, we identified and corrected a data-formatting artifact in genome identifiers. BV-BRC Genome IDs follow a <taxon_id>.<accession_number> format (e.g., 562.226210) in which the digits following the decimal point are an arbitrary identifier, not a decimal fraction. When parsed with default numeric type inference, identifiers differing only in trailing zeros (e.g., 562.22621 and 562.226210) collapse to the same floating-point value, silently merging two unrelated genomes. All Genome ID fields were therefore parsed and joined as string type throughout the pipeline; this correction was validated by confirming that genome metadata records were 100% unique by Genome ID once parsed as strings (versus 4,651 spurious duplicate identifiers among the 119,464 metadata records when parsed as floating-point numbers).

Isolation-country values of "England" were harmonized to "United Kingdom" (BV-BRC records the two inconsistently for the same underlying national jurisdiction), and a single record with the non-geographic placeholder value "Control" was treated as missing. Of the 7,274 quality-controlled genomes, 6,986 (96.0%) had a resolvable isolation country.

## 2.3 Population definition

Genomes were grouped by isolation country and cross-tabulated by ciprofloxacin phenotype (Supplementary Table S1). Three populations were used in the primary cross-population analysis: the two countries that met a minimum-size criterion (at least 100 genomes in each phenotype class) and one pooled South Asian population that did not:

- United Kingdom (training/backbone population): 2,575 genomes (731 resistant, 1,844 susceptible; collection years 2001–2012). Only the United Kingdom and Norway had at least 100 genomes in each phenotype class (Supplementary Table S1); the United Kingdom, the larger of the two, was therefore selected as the training population. One UK genome had no MLST assignment and was excluded from model training and cross-validation (Section 2.6), leaving 2,574 genomes (730 resistant, 1,844 susceptible) for those analyses.

- Norway (external test population, low resistance prevalence): 3,153 genomes (311 resistant, 2,842 susceptible; collection years 1999–2017). Ciprofloxacin resistance prevalence among these genomes (9.9%) is substantially lower than in the UK (28.4%).

- South Asia (external test population, illustrative case study): genomes from India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh were pooled because none of the three countries met the size threshold individually, yielding 238 genomes (159 resistant, 79 susceptible; collection years 2007–2021). Neither the pooled population (79 susceptible genomes) nor any single country (India: 77R/38S; Pakistan: 81R/39S; Bangladesh: 1R/2S) met the ≥100-per-class threshold, so this population is reported throughout as a smaller, illustrative case study rather than a statistically decisive test population, consistent with its wide confidence intervals below. Resistance prevalence among these genomes (66.8%) is substantially higher than in the UK.

No genome appears in more than one of the three populations, and no BioProject accession is shared between any pair of populations (checked by set intersection on non-missing accessions; Norway has only two). Prevalence figures reported for each population describe the genomes deposited in BV-BRC, which reflect the design of the contributing studies and submission patterns, and are not estimates of national resistance rates.

## 2.4 Genomic feature construction

Resistance-associated genomic features were derived from BV-BRC's Specialty Genes annotation (evidence types BLAT, K-mer Search, RGI, BLASTP, and AMRFinderPlus), restricted to the "Antibiotic Resistance" property category and retrieved for the genomes in the analytic cohort.

Two classes of feature were constructed:

***Chromosomal quinolone-resistance mutations****.* AMRFinderPlus point-mutation calls (evidence type "AMRFinderPlus: POINTP") were used to define both chromosomal features. For gyrA, a genome was scored positive if it carried at least one POINTP record whose antibiotic-class annotation was exactly "quinolone"; 31 POINTP records annotated with the combined class "quinolone/triclosan" were therefore not counted. For parC, a genome was scored positive if it carried at least one POINTP record. The resulting binary features (gyrA_fq_mut, parC_fq_mut) indicate the presence of at least one fluoroquinolone-resistance-associated point mutation in the respective gene, irrespective of the exact codon substitution; per-record amino-acid substitution identity (e.g., S83L versus D87N) is not provided as a separate field in BV-BRC's Specialty Genes export and was not extracted from free text.

***Acquired plasmid-mediated resistance genes****.* Presence of qnrA, qnrB, qnrS, oqxA, oqxB, and AAC(6′)-Ib-cr was determined by case-insensitive matching of annotation content in the Gene, Function, and Product fields of BV-BRC Specialty Genes records, with gene-specific rules. Manual review showed that the Gene field was unreliable for some qnr records (for example, QnrA1 records could be annotated as pipB2_2 in the Gene field, while the specific qnr identity was recoverable from the Function field), and that in the keyword-search exports the Product field followed the search term rather than describing the individual record (for example, records identifying QnrS1 could also carry the Product description "QnrB family"). qnrA, qnrB, and qnrS were therefore scored positive only when the pooled Gene, Function, and Product text contained an allele-level designation (qnrA\d+, qnrB\d+, or qnrS\d+, matched case-insensitively; for example QnrS1 or QnrB10), so that family-level strings such as "QnrB family" could not trigger a call. oqxA and oqxB were scored by case-insensitive matching of the gene name in the pooled text. For AAC(6′)-Ib-cr, only the Gene and Function fields were used, because Product-field descriptions produced matches to unrelated genes, including TEM-family beta-lactamases and the non-fluoroquinolone-active AAC(6′)-Ib10 allele. Original single-gene keyword-search exports were pooled across all eight searches and reclassified according to annotation content rather than source-search label, because several exports contained substantial cross-gene contamination.

The resulting feature matrix comprises eight binary (presence/absence) features per genome: gyrA_fq_mut, parC_fq_mut, qnrA, qnrB, qnrS, oqxA, oqxB, aac6_Ib_cr.

As an internal validity check, we confirmed that both chromosomal features are strongly associated with observed phenotype in the expected direction before fitting the final model, across the full 7,274-genome quality-controlled cohort: 96.64% of resistant genomes carry gyrA_fq_mut versus 7.62% of susceptible genomes, and 93.49% of resistant genomes carry parC_fq_mut versus 1.71% of susceptible genomes. Carriage of all eight features by population and phenotype is reported in Table 3, and carriage of the gyrA feature before and after correction in the UK and Norway is reported in Section 2.4.1.

### 2.4.1 Feature construction validation

Feature construction was validated against the expected biology. An initial gyrA definition based on the antibiotic-class field alone flagged 99.96% of susceptible Norwegian genomes and 39.0% of susceptible UK genomes as gyrA-positive, which is implausible for a fluoroquinolone-resistance mutation feature. Inspection of the gene_gyrA export showed that it pools five evidence types (BLAT, K-mer Search, AMRFinderPlus: POINTP, RGI protein variant model, and BLASTP). Only POINTP records represent a called point mutation; the other evidence types are homology or presence detectors that also fire on the wild-type gene, so class-only matching scored wild-type genomes as mutation-positive.

The final gyrA_fq_mut feature was therefore restricted to POINTP records with the antibiotic class "quinolone" (Section 2.4). This correction reduced gyrA_fq_mut carriage among susceptible genomes from 79.03% to 7.62% across the quality-controlled cohort (UK: 39.0% to 8.9%; Norway: 99.96% to 4.9%), while carriage among resistant genomes decreased from 99.57% to 96.64% across the cohort (after correction: 99.0% in the UK and 95.2% in Norway). The 31 quinolone/triclosan POINTP records excluded by this rule belong to 31 genomes: 20 were gyrA-positive through other records (19 resistant, 1 susceptible), and the other 11 were all susceptible, so the exclusion removed no resistant genome from the feature.

For qnrB and AAC(6′)-Ib-cr, manual review identified generic Product-field descriptions that produced false-positive classifications: in the exports searched for qnrB and ib-cr, the Product value followed the search term rather than the individual record. qnrB classification was therefore based on allele-level identity (Section 2.4), and Product was excluded for AAC(6′)-Ib-cr. Under the corrected classification, qnrB was present in 28 genomes (compared with 214 under the initial classification). Under the final classification, AAC(6′)-Ib-cr was present in 485 genomes of the quality-controlled cohort, compared with 488 under the initial classification.

The parC, oqxA, and oqxB features were also reviewed for analogous annotation problems. For parC, restricting to POINTP evidence rather than matching on the class field alone did not change carriage rates by phenotype class in the UK or Norway at three decimal places, and no comparable evidence-type or Product-field inconsistencies were identified for oqxA or oqxB. All models, metrics, and figures reported in this manuscript were regenerated with the corrected feature definitions after these checks.

## 2.5 Predictive model

Ciprofloxacin resistance was modeled as a binary outcome using elastic-net-penalized logistic regression implemented with scikit-learn's LogisticRegression (penalty="elasticnet", l1_ratio=0.5, solver="saga", C=1.0, max_iter=5000). Hyperparameters were fixed and not tuned. The model was trained exclusively on the UK backbone population (2,574 genomes with an MLST assignment), with Norway and South Asia retained as external populations for evaluation.

As a comparator, we implemented a non-probabilistic rule-based baseline that predicted "resistant" if any of the eight engineered features was present and "susceptible" otherwise. This baseline represents direct feature-based classification without fitted model coefficients or probability estimation. Because it involves no fitting, it was evaluated directly on all genomes in each population without cross-validation. Its output is binary, so its AUROC and AUPRC were computed from the binary prediction; its AUROC therefore equals the mean of its sensitivity and specificity.

## 2.6 Cross-validation strategy

In-domain (UK) performance was estimated using 5-fold cross-validation with out-of-fold predictions (sklearn.model_selection.cross_val_predict). Folds were constructed to reduce leakage between closely related genomes: rather than a simple random split, all genomes sharing the same MLST sequence type were constrained to fall entirely within a single fold. We considered grouping by BioProject accession (the submitting study); however, BioProject accession was missing for 42.4% of UK genomes and 99.9% of Norway genomes in the retrieved metadata, and every non-missing UK value was the same accession (PRJEB4681, covering 57.6% of UK genomes), making BioProject unsuitable as a grouping variable for this cohort. MLST was missing for only 1 of 2,575 UK genomes (a resistant genome), which was excluded from training and cross-validation, leaving 2,574 genomes; MLST was used as the grouping variable instead. Unstratified GroupKFold gave folds with 11.7% to 61.2% resistant genomes, so StratifiedGroupKFold (shuffle = True, random_state = 42, k = 5) was used to limit class imbalance while keeping each sequence type within one fold. Imbalance remained (9.7% to 49.9% resistant per fold; 28.4% overall), in part because a single, predominantly resistant sequence type 131 [ST131] cluster of 395 genomes must fall within one fold. The absence of shared sequence types between the training and validation portions of every fold was confirmed post hoc.

For external evaluation, a final model was refit on the entire UK backbone (n = 2,574) and applied without any refitting to the Norway and South Asia populations, which were not used at any stage of model training or cross-validation, although their feature carriage prompted the correction of the gyrA definition (Section 2.4.1).

## 2.7 Discrimination metrics

Discriminative performance was summarized using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), sensitivity, specificity, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). MCC was included because it incorporates all four components of the confusion matrix and remains informative under class imbalance. Sensitivity, specificity, and MCC were computed at a fixed probability threshold of 0.5. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for all discrimination metrics were obtained by non-parametric bootstrap resampling of each evaluation dataset (1,000 resamples with replacement, percentile method, random seed 0). Point estimates are those computed on the full evaluation set, and intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution; resamples containing a single phenotype class were skipped. Resampling was performed at the level of individual genomes and does not account for lineage clustering.

## 2.8 Calibration assessment

Calibration, defined as the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcome frequencies, was assessed separately from discrimination, informed by the calibration framework of Van Calster *et al.* (2016). For each population, we computed:

- Calibration slope and intercept, estimated jointly using a logistic calibration model (effectively unpenalized, C = 10⁶) in which the binary outcome was regressed on the log-odds of the model's predicted probability. Predicted probabilities were clipped to [10⁻⁶, 1−10⁻⁶] to avoid undefined log-odds at the boundaries. A calibration slope of 1 and intercept of 0 indicate perfect calibration; a slope below 1 indicates predictions that are too extreme. Because slope and intercept were estimated jointly, the intercept is the log-odds of resistance at a predicted probability of 0.5, not a calibration-in-the-large estimate with the slope fixed at 1, and it should be read together with the slope.

- Brier score, defined as the mean squared error between predicted probability and observed binary outcome.

- Expected Calibration Error (ECE), computed using 10 equal-width probability bins.

Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for all calibration metrics were obtained using the same non-parametric bootstrap procedure described in §2.7.

Because the model uses only eight binary input features, only a small number of distinct predicted-probability values occurred in practice, reflecting the feature combinations observed in each population. We therefore additionally report an exact reliability table (Supplementary Table S2) showing the observed resistance frequency at each distinct predicted-probability value produced by the model.

## 2.9 Recalibration experiment

To evaluate whether calibration could be improved using a limited amount of target-population data, we performed Platt scaling, defined here as logistic recalibration of the log-odds of the original model's predictions, using varying amounts of Norway or South Asia data. For each target population and each recalibration sample size N ∈ {25, 50, 100, 150, 200}, we performed 300 random repetitions. In each repetition, N genomes were sampled at random without replacement from the target population as a recalibration set. A single-variable logistic regression (slope and intercept, effectively unpenalized) was then fitted to the log-odds of the original model's predictions for these genomes against their true labels. The fitted recalibration mapping was applied to the remaining genomes in the same population, which served as the held-out evaluation set. Calibration intercept (estimated as in Section 2.8) and Brier score were then computed on the held-out set. Pre-recalibration values (Table 7) are those of the original model evaluated on the full target population and were not recomputed on each held-out set.

Repetitions in which either the recalibration set or the held-out evaluation set contained only one phenotype class were discarded, as required for fitting and evaluating the logistic recalibration model. This occurred primarily at N=25 in Norway; South Asia's more balanced class distribution meant discards were rare or absent at every sample size. Discarded repetitions were replaced by further draws (up to 100 additional attempts per condition) so that each condition comprised 300 valid repetitions. Each repetition used an independently seeded random number generator with seed 1000N + repetition index (the index counts all attempts, including discarded ones) to ensure that the sampling procedure was exactly reproducible.

For the recalibration experiment, results were reported as the median and interquartile range (IQR) rather than the mean and percentile interval. This choice was made after mean-based summaries proved unstable in the South Asia N = 200 condition, which exhibited near-separation: 17 of 300 repetitions produced extreme calibration-intercept estimates (|intercept| > 5), as low as −38.76, from the unregularized logistic fit on only 38 held-out genomes.

As a post hoc sensitivity analysis, the experiment was repeated with a ridge penalty (C = 1, the scikit-learn default) on both logistic fits, alongside the unpenalized fits (C = 10⁶), on identical splits. Splits in this analysis were drawn by permuting the target population with random seed 1000 × repetition index + N and taking the first N genomes as the recalibration set; repetitions with a single phenotype class in either set were discarded and not replaced, so some conditions have fewer than 300 repetitions (281 in Norway at N = 25 and 297 at N = 50).

## 2.10 Resistance-mechanism and lineage analysis

To describe resistance-mechanism composition, resistant genomes in each population were classified into three mutually exclusive categories based on their engineered feature values. Genomes carrying a parC fluoroquinolone-resistance mutation were classified as parC mutation, regardless of whether other features were also present; genomes without a parC mutation but carrying at least one acquired resistance gene (qnrA, qnrB, qnrS, oqxA, oqxB, or aac6_Ib_cr) were classified as acquired gene; and genomes carrying neither a parC mutation nor any of these acquired resistance genes were classified as neither. The trained model's mean predicted probability was compared across these three mechanism categories within each population.

Lineage composition was characterized using the number of distinct MLST sequence types, the proportion of each population belonging to sequence type 131 (ST131), the resistance rate among ST131 genomes, and the combined share of the five most frequent sequence types (top-5-lineage share), for each of the three study populations.

## 2.11 Post hoc comparator and feature-level analyses

Two analyses were added after the primary results had been examined and are reported as post hoc. First, to test whether the zero coefficients for qnrA, qnrS, oqxA, and oqxB reflected the elastic-net penalty or the rarity of these features, the eight-feature model was refit on the 2,574 UK genomes without regularization (penalty = none, solver = lbfgs, max_iter = 5000), and convergence warnings were recorded. Second, to quantify what the seven features other than parC_fq_mut contribute, an unpenalized logistic regression on parC_fq_mut alone was evaluated with the same MLST-grouped folds as the primary model (Section 2.6), refit on the 2,574 UK genomes, and applied without refitting to Norway and South Asia. AUROC, MCC at a probability threshold of 0.5, and the calibration slope and intercept (Section 2.8) were compared with the eight-feature model. The differences in AUROC and MCC between the two models were summarized with a paired bootstrap (1,000 resamples, random seed 0, percentile intervals) in which both models were evaluated on the same resampled genomes.

## 2.12 Post hoc sensitivity analysis: collection period

The three populations were collected over different periods (UK 2001–2012, Norway 1999–2017, South Asia 2007–2021), so the primary comparison confounds geography with time. To probe this, the trained model was applied without refitting to Norwegian and South Asian genomes collected in 2007–2012, the years covered by all three populations. For each subset we report the number of genomes, resistance prevalence, mean predicted probability, AUROC, and calibration slope and intercept (Section 2.8), with 95% bootstrap intervals (Section 2.7) for subsets of at least 100 genomes; smaller subsets are described by point estimates only. Mechanism composition among resistant genomes (Section 2.10) and the ST131 share were compared with the full Norwegian population. The UK training population was not restricted, but the number of UK genomes collected in the window is reported for reference. Genomes without a collection year were excluded from the window subsets.

The coefficients of qnrA, qnrS, oqxA, and oqxB were zero: oqxA and oqxB had no carriers among UK genomes, qnrA had one (susceptible), and qnrS was carried by 28 UK genomes, none of them resistant (Table 3). In this model a genome with a parC mutation and no other feature scores 0.488, below the 0.5 threshold, and a genome with both parC and gyrA mutations scores 0.976. Because the parC-only score sits just below the threshold, sensitivity, specificity, and MCC at 0.5 depend on the threshold choice, whereas AUROC does not. The size of the parC_fq_mut coefficient relative to all others means that the model's output is driven mainly by parC status. Section 3.7 examines whether the zero coefficients reflect the penalty and how a model using parC_fq_mut alone compares.

## 3.4 Resistance-mechanism composition differs across populations

We described the resistance mechanisms of truly resistant genomes in each population (Table 6). A parC mutation was present in 98.5% of resistant UK genomes (719/730), 88.1% of resistant Norwegian genomes (274/311), and 94.3% of resistant South Asian genomes (150/159). Resistance attributable only to an acquired plasmid-mediated gene, without a parC mutation, was rare: 0.1% in the UK (1 genome), 1.3% in Norway (4 genomes), and 4.4% in South Asia (7 genomes). The model's mean predicted probability for these genomes ranged from 0.041 to 0.134 across populations, compared with approximately 0.98 for parC-mutation genomes; because the acquired-gene-only groups contain only 1, 4, and 7 genomes, differences between populations within this range should not be interpreted. All 7 South Asian and 3 of the 4 Norwegian acquired-gene-only genomes carried qnrS (the fourth Norwegian genome carried aac6_Ib_cr), and the single UK genome carried qnrB.

The largest group of resistant genomes not explained by the modeled mechanisms was in Norway: 33 resistant genomes (10.6%) carried neither a parC mutation nor any acquired resistance gene, compared with 10 (1.4%) in the UK and 2 (1.3%) in South Asia, and the model assigned them a mean predicted probability of 0.042. These 33 Norwegian genomes were distributed across 17 MLST types, the largest of which (ST131) accounted for 24% of the group, so the finding is not attributable to a single dominant clone, but 30 of the 33 were collected in 2013–2017 (Section 3.8). Of the 33, 19 carried gyrA_fq_mut only and 14 carried none of the eight features (Table 3). Together with the 4 acquired-gene-only genomes, they account for all 37 resistant Norwegian genomes classified as susceptible at the 0.5 threshold.

The miscalibration at the low end of the predicted range consists of resistant genomes without a parC mutation. Among genomes with a predicted probability below 0.1 (Supplementary Table S2), observed resistance was 0.013 in Norway (n = 2,822; mean predicted probability 0.005) and 0.103 in South Asia (9 of 87 genomes; mean predicted probability 0.034). Every resistant genome in these groups lacks a parC mutation, because no genome with a parC mutation receives a probability below 0.488, and the 9 resistant South Asian genomes in this group are the 7 acquired-gene-only and 2 neither genomes described above.

This analysis is descriptive. It shows that mechanism composition differs between populations, most clearly in the Norwegian "neither" group, but it does not quantify how much of the calibration shift in either external population is attributable to genomes in any one mechanism category.

## 3.5 Recalibration with local data moves the intercept toward zero, with mixed effects on Brier score

Table 7 and Figure 3 summarize the recalibration experiment. Results are reported as medians and interquartile ranges, as specified in Section 2.9. Before-recalibration values are those of the original model on the full target population; after-recalibration values are computed on the genomes held out from each recalibration sample, so the comparison is indicative rather than paired.

In Norway, the median post-recalibration intercept was −0.596 at N = 25, further from zero than the pre-recalibration value (−0.488), and approached zero as N increased (−0.036 at N = 150 and −0.034 at N = 200). The IQR was widest at N = 50 and 100 (widths 1.23 and 1.27) and 0.84 at N = 200. The median Brier score was above the pre-recalibration value at N = 25 and 50 (0.0220 and 0.0208 versus 0.0206) and slightly below it from N = 100 (0.0200 to 0.0196).

In South Asia, the median intercept was −0.412 at N = 25, opposite in sign to the pre-recalibration value (0.927), and was 0.039 at N = 150 and 0.249 at N = 200. The IQR widened sharply at N = 200 (−3.265 to 0.907): 17 of 300 repetitions produced extreme estimates (|intercept| > 5) because only 38 of the 238 genomes remained for held-out evaluation. Extreme estimates also occurred at smaller sample sizes: in Norway at N = 25 (9 of 300) and in South Asia at N = 25, 50, 100, and 150 (8, 5, 1, and 1 of 300). The median Brier score was above the pre-recalibration value (0.0399) at N = 25 to 150 (0.0429 to 0.0469) and below it only at N = 200 (0.0368), the condition with the fewest held-out genomes and the least stable intercept.

The extreme estimates depend on the estimator. In a post hoc sensitivity analysis on identical random splits (Section 2.9), a ridge penalty (C = 1) on both logistic fits gave median intercepts within 0.20 of zero at every N in both populations (Norway −0.120 to 0.191; South Asia −0.054 to 0.040) and removed the extreme estimates in South Asia (none of 300 with |intercept| > 5 at any N, against 16, 12, 1, 0, and 12 of 300 at N = 25, 50, 100, 150, and 200 without the penalty); one extreme estimate remained in Norway at N = 25. Without the penalty, the same splits reproduced the pattern in Table 7 (Norway at N = 25: median −0.596; South Asia at N = 200: median 0.233, with 12 of 300 estimates beyond ±5).

Recalibration on local samples therefore brought the median intercept closer to zero in South Asia at every N and in Norway from N = 50, but it did not consistently improve the Brier score. The slow approach to zero at small N in Table 7 and the instability at N = 200 in South Asia reflect the unpenalized estimator on few events; the conclusion that a modest local sample brings the intercept close to zero holds under both estimators (from about 100 to 150 genomes without the penalty, and from 25 with it).

## 3.6 Lineage composition differs across populations

Sequence type 131 (ST131), a globally disseminated fluoroquinolone-resistance-associated clone, was represented at markedly different frequencies across the three populations: 15.3% of the UK population (395/2,574), 7.9% of Norway (250/3,153), and 5.0% of South Asia (12/238). Resistance rate within ST131 specifically also differed by population (UK: 74.9%; Norway: 50.0%; South Asia: 91.7% [12 genomes]), indicating that even within a single, globally distributed lineage, resistance rates differed between the populations; the South Asian figure rests on 12 genomes, and all three comparisons are subject to the same confounding by collection period and study composition as the rest of the analysis. The number of distinct MLST types was 274 in the UK, 407 in Norway, and 79 in South Asia, but these counts depend on sample size and are not comparable as measures of diversity. The share of each population in its five most frequent lineages was 41.3% in the UK, 47.7% in Norway, and 29.4% in South Asia.

## 3.8 Post hoc sensitivity analysis: collection period

Geography and collection period are partly confounded (Section 3.1). To test whether this explains the Norwegian calibration shift, the comparison was repeated within 2007–2012, the years covered by all three populations (Section 2.12; Table 10). The window contained 884 UK genomes (180 resistant, 20.4%), compared with 28.4% resistant in the full UK population.

In Norway, restricting to the window gave a slightly higher AUROC (0.986; 95% CI 0.967–0.999) whose interval overlaps the full-population interval (0.954–0.981), and moved the calibration slope from 0.726 to 1.003, but that interval is wide (0.814–5.303) because the window holds 88 resistant genomes. The intercept stayed negative and was more negative than in the full population (−1.464; −17.429 to −0.819). Mean predicted probability was 0.091 against an observed prevalence of 0.078 in the window, compared with 0.102 against 0.099 in the full population. Mechanism composition changed markedly: 97.7% of resistant Norwegian genomes in the window carried a parC mutation and 2.3% (2 genomes) fell in the neither category, compared with 88.1% and 10.6% (33 genomes) in the full population, so 31 of the 33 genomes not explained by the modeled features were collected outside 2007–2012. The ST131 share was 6.7% in the window and 7.9% in the full population.

By period, the 311 resistant Norwegian genomes fall into two groups: 116 collected in 1999–2012, a range that includes the UK training years (2001–2012), and 195 collected in 2013–2017. A parC mutation was present in 113 of the 116 (97.4%) and 161 of the 195 (82.6%). Genomes with neither a parC mutation nor an acquired gene numbered 3 of 116 (2.6%) in the earlier period and 30 of 195 (15.4%) in the later one, and all 4 genomes with an acquired gene but no parC mutation were collected in 2013–2017 (2.1%).

In South Asia, the window held 62 genomes, only 8 of them resistant (12.9%), compared with 66.8% resistant in the full population. Only 8 of the 159 resistant genomes were collected in 2007–2012, against 54 of the 79 susceptible genomes. In this window the mean predicted probability (0.151) exceeded the observed prevalence (0.129) and the calibration intercept was −0.963, the opposite sign to the full population (0.927). With 8 resistant genomes the intercept and mean-probability estimates are too imprecise to interpret, and no intervals are reported. The window's AUROC (1.000) and calibration slope (3.254; Table 10) reflect the same 8 resistant genomes: the model separates them perfectly from the 54 susceptible genomes in the window, and fitting an unpenalized calibration model (Section 2.8) to perfectly separated data does not have a well-defined maximum-likelihood solution, so the reported slope should not be read as a stable estimate. The prevalence, however, differs sharply from the full population, so the high prevalence observed in South Asia depends on which years, and which studies, are included.

South Asian phenotype closely followed the study of origin. The genomes came from four BioProjects and a group without an accession. The two 2007–2010 studies (PRJEB2827 and PRJEB2226) contributed 62 genomes, 8 of them resistant; a 2015–2017 study (PRJNA522294) contributed 85 genomes, 67 of them resistant; one study without collection years (PRJNA925003) contributed 35 genomes, all resistant; and the 56 genomes without an accession included 49 resistant genomes. In total, 56 South Asian genomes have no collection year (the 35 from PRJNA925003 and 21 without an accession) and are excluded from the window. In both external populations, collection period is therefore not separable from the geographic comparison in these data.
